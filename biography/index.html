Biography.

I come from Sichuan, a province known for its Taoist roots: seeking inner peace and a self that lies beyond social conventions, in what Taoists call Nature (自然). I won’t claim this has much in common with Stoicism or Cynicism. I’d just like to.

Over the past twenty years, a growing number of pandas have watched my hometown modernize at breakneck speed, and my memories of it have blurred next to what I see now. Through the ’90s and early 2000s, my elementary and middle schools were covered in slogans that were both highly motivated and slightly far-fetched. Ranking top of the class was all you needed, long before attention was all the AIs needed. It wasn’t easy, but it wasn’t that hard either. Ninety-five out of a hundred in any of the nine subjects? Easy peasy. I never once wondered why anything happened or where life might lead. Looking back, the world at the time held exactly two countries, ideologically speaking: China, and “The Foreign Country.”

Then came 2008. On May 12th, a magnitude 8.0 earthquake struck about 240 km from where I was. That was when the idea of Death first ambushed me, quietly. Buildings swayed by something like ten degrees. That night was filled with muffled weeping around me and a ground that wouldn’t stop trembling, which, oddly, reminded me of nothing more than an overnight train to somewhere far away. The earthquake was also my first real encounter with geology, geophysics, or geoscience of any kind. That’s despite the fact that, like every child, I’d always answered the career question with “Scientist.” My high school geography teacher (a subject now renamed Geoscience, which is relevant) once made a brilliant pun about how Geography, then filed under the humanities, evolves into Geophysics and becomes a STEM subject. I’m sorry to say it doesn’t survive translation, and I’ll never forgive myself.

At Tongji University in Shanghai, I majored in Geology and Marine Science. Geology turned out to be genuinely fun, especially fieldwork, when the workload was light. Fieldwork takes you to places that are, let’s say, not exactly tourist destinations, which is a blessing when you live in a metropolis packed with people around the clock. I was hopeless in the lab, though, even in Mineralogy, supposedly the easiest of the lot (sorry). I could ace the exams; I just couldn’t do the actual work. So switching to models seemed like the sensible move.

For me, the real beauty of carbon cycle research is how unpredictable and counterintuitive it is. People keep asking me: if the AMOC strengthens, weakens, or collapses, what happens to the ocean carbon sink, and to atmospheric CO₂? I hope to answer that someday. My honest view for now is that there may be no clean answer, because everything in the system is hopelessly tangled up with everything else. In practical terms, this means the NSFC (or any funding agency, really) should keep funding me no matter what. Philosophically, it actually makes sense to me. The past century has planted a deep skepticism in the natural sciences, and we’re learning to live with uncertainty, and with uncertainty about our uncertainty. I take it as a gift.

For a long stretch of my life, I was badly addicted to video games, mostly Dota 2. It’s still my favorite, but these days I’m too rusty, and too lazy, to grind ranked. That said, if you’re an Immortal player, I’m more than happy to be carried.

I’m a K-pop fan, and I also listen to a lot of Mozart and Bach.

I love reading. You’ll find a list on my WeChat account. Keep reading.

Cheers.
